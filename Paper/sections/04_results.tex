\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{A low-dimensional consumer-resource dynamical model predicts combinatorial microbial assembly independent of richness and unobserved species pairings}
\label{subsec:venturelli}

Predicting how multispecies microbial communities assemble from individual constituent taxa is a central challenge in microbiome engineering and synthetic biology. Standard modeling approaches frequently rely on Generalized Lotka-Volterra (gLV) equations, which parameterize all pairwise interactions via an $S \times S$ interaction matrix~\cite{venturelli2018deciphering,may1972will}. However, gLV formulations suffer from quadratic parameter scaling, severe risk of overfitting, and an inability to generalize when species are introduced into combinations or physical regimes outside the training distribution~\cite{barbier2020generic,goldford2018emergent}. 

Here, we demonstrate that a coarse-grained, low-dimensional Consumer-Resource Model (CRM)~\cite{macarthur1970species,tilman1982resource} provides a powerful, parameter-efficient framework capable of accurately capturing multispecies assembly dynamics. Rather than fitting $S^2$ phenomenological interaction coefficients, the model projects species interactions onto a low-dimensional latent metabolic resource space ($K \ll S$), governed by:
\begin{align}
\frac{dN_i}{dt} &= N_i \sum_{k=1}^K R_k \Theta_{ik} - \alpha_i N_i^2, \label{eq:crm_N} \\
\frac{dR_k}{dt} &= - c \, R_k \sum_{i=1}^S N_i \Theta_{ik} + \sum_{l=1}^K \left( c \, R_l \sum_{i=1}^S N_i \Theta_{il} \right) W_{lk}, \label{eq:crm_R}
\end{align}
where $N_i(t)$ represents the abundance of species $i$, $R_k(t)$ denotes the concentration of latent resource $k$, $\Theta_{ik} \ge 0$ is the metabolic consumption affinity matrix, $\alpha_i > 0$ parameterizes intraspecific crowding, $c$ is the base uptake rate, and $W_{lk} \ge 0$ represents the stoichiometric byproduct cross-feeding matrix satisfying physical mass-conservation constraints ($\sum_k W_{lk} \le 1$).

We first evaluated this framework on an extensive combinatorial dataset comprising $S=12$ prevalent human gut isolates cultured across 175 distinct synthetic communities (monocultures, pairs, trios, and higher-order consortia up to 12 species) sampled longitudinally across 6 timepoints over 60 hours (Venturelli et al.~\cite{venturelli2018deciphering}). The dataset was partitioned into 102 training communities and 73 strictly held-out test communities. To prevent data leakage, we selected the optimal model architecture and hyperparameters via a systematic 5-fold cross-validation conducted strictly within the 102 training communities, evaluating held-out discrete Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence on the probability simplex $\Delta^{11}$ alongside absolute biomass accuracy across $K \in \{2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8\}$ and byproduct cross-feeding bounds $c_w \in \{0.20, 0.40, 0.60, 0.80\}$. Held-out validation KL divergence reached its global minimum at $K = 3$ latent resources ($c_w = 0.20$; $\text{Held-Out KL} = 0.2193 \pm 0.0085$ nats, validation Abs $R^2 = 0.7849 \pm 0.0069$, Rel $R^2 = 0.7877 \pm 0.0075$), significantly outperforming higher-dimensional formulations while reducing total parameter count by $>59\%$ relative to unconstrained baselines (61 parameters for $K=3$ vs. 152 for $K=7$). When trained on the full 102 training communities, the $K=3$ physical Neural ODE achieved robust in-sample calibration ($R^2 = 0.830$, Pearson $r = 0.912$, $N = 6,120$ points).

When evaluated on the 73 strictly held-out test consortia across all dynamic timepoints ($t \in [12, 24, 36, 48, 60]$\,h), the frozen $K=3$ model demonstrated remarkable out-of-sample generalization (Figure~\ref{fig:ophelia_assembly}A), attaining an overall test $R^2 = 0.766$ and Pearson $r = 0.877$ on absolute $\text{OD}_{600}$ ($N = 4,380$ points, $\text{MSE} = 0.00393$), with relative abundance correlation reaching $r = 0.899$ and relative $R^2 = 0.802$ ($0.1888 \pm 0.0015$ nats across an $N=10$ random-seed ensemble). Crucially, high predictive accuracy was maintained across both early exponential growth and late stationary saturation ($t = 12\text{h}: R^2 = 0.726,\ r = 0.856$; $t = 24\text{h}: R^2 = 0.799,\ r = 0.895$; $t = 36\text{h}: R^2 = 0.766,\ r = 0.884$; $t = 48\text{h}: R^2 = 0.789,\ r = 0.890$; $t = 60\text{h}: R^2 = 0.749,\ r = 0.866$).

To assess whether the model learned generalizable metabolic principles rather than memorizing co-occurrence statistics, we conducted two rigorous stress tests:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Invariance to Community Diversity and Compositional Novelty (Negative Results):} We evaluated whether model accuracy deteriorated as communities became more complex or diverged from the training set (Figure~\ref{fig:ophelia_assembly}B--C). In contrast to phenomenological models where pairwise interaction errors compound with community size, trajectory prediction accuracy showed no significant correlation with initial community Shannon diversity ($r = -0.125,\ p = 0.292$; Figure~\ref{fig:ophelia_assembly}B) or initial species richness ($r = -0.141,\ p = 0.234$). Furthermore, evaluating accuracy against compositional distance to the training set revealed complete invariance: the correlation with mean Bray-Curtis dissimilarity was virtually zero ($r = +0.036,\ p = 0.765$; Figure~\ref{fig:ophelia_assembly}C), with identical invariance observed across minimal Bray-Curtis distance, Jaccard distance, and Jensen-Shannon divergence.
    \item \textbf{Generalization to Unobserved Species Pairings (Leave-Pair-Out Benchmark):} To verify that the inferred latent parameters ($\mathbf{\Theta}, W$) capture transferable biochemical traits rather than empirical correlations, we performed a leave-pair-out benchmark across all 66 possible species pairs. We trained 66 independent models, in each case strictly withholding all communities containing a specific pair $(i, j)$ from the training data. When evaluated on the held-out consortia containing these unobserved pairings, the low-dimensional CRM successfully predicted their co-culture assembly, attaining a mean per-pair held-out $R^2 = 0.874 \pm 0.061$ and an overall pooled cross-pair $R^2 = 0.628$ with Pearson $r = 0.806$ across $N = 71,340$ points ($t = 12\text{h}: R^2 = 0.966 \pm 0.011$; $t = 24\text{h}: R^2 = 0.937 \pm 0.028$; $t = 36\text{h}: R^2 = 0.881 \pm 0.059$; $t = 48\text{h}: R^2 = 0.832 \pm 0.108$; $t = 60\text{h}: R^2 = 0.756 \pm 0.147$).
\end{enumerate}
These results establish that low-dimensional CRMs do not suffer from the error accumulation that plagues phenomenological gLV models in high-diversity consortia, providing a scalable foundation for predicting community assembly.



\subsection{Modular adaptation to structured microenvironments captures biofilm-liquid partitioning with universal metabolic parameters}
\label{subsec:pollard_adaptation}

Having established the predictive power of the low-dimensional CRM in well-mixed cultures, we asked whether the framework could be generalized to complex, spatially structured microenvironments without abandoning its core physical principles.

In natural mammalian niches, gut microbiota do not inhabit well-mixed liquid cultures; rather, they partition between the mucosal surface layer and the luminal fluid~\cite{jin2023culturing}. To determine whether a low-dimensional CRM can model such spatial partitioning, we adapted our framework to analyze a longitudinal dataset from Jin et al.~\cite{jin2023culturing}, where a 50-strain synthetic human gut community was serially passaged over 6 serial dilution cycles (18 days) under three distinct physical regimes: (i) liquid media only (\textit{No Carrier}), (ii) liquid media supplemented with inert 1\% agarose hydrogel beads (\textit{Plain Agar}), and (iii) liquid media supplemented with 0.5\% porcine gastric mucin embedded in 1\% agarose beads (\textit{Mucin Agar}). Each experimental regime comprised $N=3$ independent biological culture vessels ($R_1, R_2, R_3$). In the original study, each vessel was destructively sub-sampled across 3 spatial bead and supernatant aliquots ($a, b, c$) at each passage; because beads are sacrificed upon sampling, $R_{1a}$ at passage $P_2$ is not the clonal descendant of $R_{1a}$ at $P_1$. We therefore averaged over the spatial sub-samples ($a, b, c$) to define the true continuous biological trajectory for each independent culture vessel, eliminating technical pseudoreplication.

We formalized this modular adaptation by explicitly coupling two spatial compartments—the hydrogel carrier biofilm ($c$) and the surrounding liquid supernatant ($s$)—via biophysical exchange kinetics:
\begin{align}
\frac{dN_i^{(c)}}{dt} &= N_i^{(c)} \sum_{k=1}^K R_k^{(c)} \Theta_{ik}^{(c)} - \alpha_i (N_i^{(c)})^2 \nonumber \\
&\quad + k_{\text{attach}, i} N_i^{(s)} - k_{\text{shed}, i} N_i^{(c)}, \label{eq:coupled_c} \\
\frac{dN_i^{(s)}}{dt} &= N_i^{(s)} \sum_{k=1}^K R_k^{(s)} \Theta_{ik}^{(s)} - \alpha_i (N_i^{(s)})^2 \nonumber \\
&\quad - k_{\text{attach}, i} N_i^{(s)} + k_{\text{shed}, i} N_i^{(c)}, \label{eq:coupled_s}
\end{align}
where $k_{\text{attach}, i}$ and $k_{\text{shed}, i}$ denote strain-specific attachment and shedding rate constants ($\text{day}^{-1}$), and the resources $R_k^{(c)}$ and $R_k^{(s)}$ follow corresponding local depletion and byproduct generation dynamics.

We tested whether metabolic resource utilization is an intrinsic, conserved property of each bacterial genome by enforcing \textbf{parameter universality}: the metabolic consumption affinities $\mathbf{\Theta}^{(c)}, \mathbf{\Theta}^{(s)}$ and byproduct matrix $W$ (Figure~\ref{fig:pollard_unified}a) were constrained to be identical across all three experimental conditions, requiring all environmental variation to be mediated solely by physical boundary conditions ($k_{\text{attach}}, k_{\text{shed}}$ and carrier presence). Furthermore, to prevent unphysical parameter compensation, liquid carryover between passages was fixed strictly to the experimental dilution ratio ($\eta = 1/30 \approx 0.0333$).

Because microbiome relative abundances reside on the probability simplex $\Delta^{N-1}$, we selected the optimal model architecture and hyperparameters using \textbf{held-out Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence} across a systematic 3-fold leave-one-vessel-out cross-validation covering $K \in \{2, 3, 4, 5\}$, byproduct cross-feeding bounds ($c_w \in \{0.50, 0.85\}$), and carryover regimes ($\eta \in \{\text{learned}, \text{fixed } 1/30\}$). Held-out KL divergence decreased sharply from $K=2$ ($\text{KL} = 0.168 \pm 0.011$ nats) and $K=3$ ($\text{KL} = 0.153 \pm 0.013$ nats) to $K=4$ ($\text{KL} = 0.1287 \pm 0.0137$ nats), reaching its global minimum at $K=5$ ($\text{KL} = 0.1278 \pm 0.0093$ nats with $c_w = 0.50$ and fixed physical carryover $\eta = 1/30$; held-out $R^2 = 0.8559 \pm 0.0190$, $\text{MAE} = 0.0068$). Fixing the liquid dilution to its physical value ($\eta = 1/30$) prevented artificial fluid carryover from masking biological adhesion, ensuring parameter identifiability and robust generalization.

On the continuous biological vessels, the optimal $K=5$ coupled CRM achieved exceptional predictive fidelity simultaneously across all three regimes over the entire 18-day experimental horizon ($R^2_{\text{global}} = 0.887$, $\text{MAE} = 0.0058$; Plain Agar $R^2 = 0.914$, Mucin Agar $R^2 = 0.922$, No Carrier $R^2 = 0.816$). To verify that this predictive power stems from biologically meaningful parameters rather than overparameterization, we evaluated the universal model against a hierarchy of null and ablated models (Figure~\ref{fig:pollard_unified}b). The universal CRM dramatically outperformed simple persistence baselines ($R^2 = 0.139\text{--}0.599$) and models with randomly shuffled metabolic affinities $\mathbf{\Theta}$ ($R^2 = -0.434\text{ to }+0.168$). Furthermore, ablating byproduct cross-feeding ($W = 0$) degraded predictive fidelity (No Carrier $R^2$ dropping to $0.770$), while scrambling initial resource allocations ($R_0$) or physical adhesion kinetics severely diminished accuracy (Plain $R^2$ dropping to $0.743\text{--}0.821$, Mucin $R^2$ dropping to $0.771\text{--}0.790$), demonstrating that all inferred components are biologically indispensable.

Crucially, the learned parameters provide direct \textbf{mechanistic validation} of the experimental physics rather than merely serving as fitting variables:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Emergent Bimodality Reflects Transfer Mechanics:} When we analyzed the distribution of the adhesion partitioning ratio $\log_{10}(k_{\text{attach}}/k_{\text{shed}})$, the community partitioned into two distinct ecological guilds: Planktonic Opportunists (Mode 1: $\text{ratio} < 1$) and Sessile Biofilm Specialists (Mode 2: $\text{ratio} > 1$; Figure~\ref{fig:pollard_unified}c).
    \item This bimodality directly reflects the \textbf{asymmetric physical transfer protocol} employed by Jin et al.~\cite{jin2023culturing}: in each passage, beads were transferred manually with tweezers ($\sim 20\%$ physical bead retention), whereas liquid supernatant was diluted $1:30$ into fresh medium ($\sim 3.3\%$ liquid retention). This sharp step-function in washout mortality imposes intense disruptive selection: strains must either optimize for bead adhesion to exploit the $20\%$ physical lifeboat, or optimize for rapid planktonic growth in the broth. Intermediate generalists fall into a "fitness valley" and are competitively excluded.
    \item \textbf{Biochemical Validation of Mucin Recruitment:} In Plain Agar (inert hydrogel), 28.5\% of strains occupied the planktonic mode and 71.5\% occupied the biofilm mode (Figure~\ref{fig:pollard_unified}c, left). Upon the introduction of mucin, 69.0\% of the community established in the mucosal biofilm specialist guild with a statistically significant bimodal distribution (Gaussian Mixture Model $\Delta\text{BIC} = -4.88$; Figure~\ref{fig:pollard_unified}c, right; Mean $\log_{10}\text{ratio} = +0.205$). This quantitatively confirms that while plain agarose offers only generic physical adhesion, mucin glycans provide a biochemical anchor and local nutrient source that actively recruits the mucosal biofilm guild.
\end{itemize}



\subsection{Physical-metabolic decoupling exposes an evolutionary trade-off between surface adhesion and metabolic reprogramming}
\label{subsec:pollard_tradeoff}

A fundamental limitation of empirical metagenomic profiling in spatial environments is that relative abundance ratios ($\frac{N_{\text{carrier}}}{N_{\text{supernatant}}}$) conflate two fundamentally distinct biological mechanisms:
1. Did a bacterial strain enrich on the carrier because it possesses high mechanical adherence (high $k_{\text{attach}}$, low $k_{\text{shed}}$)?
2. Or did it enrich because it underwent metabolic specialization to outgrow competitors on local mucin glycans ($\mathbf{\Theta}^{(c)}$ vs. $\mathbf{\Theta}^{(s)}$)?

By explicitly uncoupling physical transport rates from metabolic utilization matrices, our coupled CRM allows us to independently quantify physical adherence affinity in each microenvironment ($k_{\text{attach}}^{\text{mucin}}/k_{\text{shed}}$ for mucin agar and $k_{\text{attach}}^{\text{plain}}/k_{\text{shed}}$ for plain agar) and metabolic shift magnitude ($\|\mathbf{\Theta}^{(c)}\| / \|\mathbf{\Theta}^{(s)}\|$) for every strain in the community.

To evaluate whether gut microbes exhibit coordinated evolutionary strategies along these two axes, we performed a multi-initialization ensemble analysis ($N=10$ independent random seeds) with full 2D error bars (Mean $\pm$ SEM) for all 50 strains on the mucosal mucin carrier (Figure~\ref{fig:pollard_unified}d). 

This analysis revealed a statistically significant, previously unknown \textbf{evolutionary trade-off} between physical adhesion affinity on mucin and metabolic specialization ($\text{Ensemble Mean } r = -0.547,\ p = 3.91 \times 10^{-5}$; Spearman $\rho = -0.615,\ p = 1.99 \times 10^{-6}$; Linear fit: $\text{Metabolic\_Shift} = -0.531 \times \log_{10}(k_{\text{att}}/k_{\text{shed}}) + 0.915$, $R^2 = 0.300$, $\text{SE} = 0.117$; Figure~\ref{fig:pollard_unified}d):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Strong Mechanical Adherers (The "Stickers"):} Strains with high physical adherence ($k_{\text{attach}}^{\text{mucin}}/k_{\text{shed}} > 1$, including mucosal \textit{Bacteroides} such as \textit{Bacteroides sp. 2-1-22}, \textit{Coprococcus comes}, and \textit{Desulfovibrio piger}) exhibited minimal metabolic divergence between the carrier and liquid phases ($\|\mathbf{\Theta}^{(c)}\| / \|\mathbf{\Theta}^{(s)}\| \approx 1$). These taxa possess specialized surface appendages, extracellular polysaccharides (such as BGC-group 157 and 120 identified by Jin et al.~\cite{jin2023culturing}), and pili that secure physical attachment. They dominate the mucosal biofilm primarily by hitchhiking on the physical retention of the beads without needing to fundamentally rewire their core metabolic machinery.
    \item \textbf{Weak Adherers (The "Metabolic Specialists"):} Conversely, strains with low mechanical adherence ($k_{\text{attach}}^{\text{mucin}}/k_{\text{shed}} < 0.5$) face continuous physical shedding into the supernatant. These strains can only colonize the carrier niche if they undergo extensive metabolic reprogramming ($\|\mathbf{\Theta}^{(c)}\| / \|\mathbf{\Theta}^{(s)}\| \gg 1$), specializing heavily in the enzymatic degradation of localized mucin glycans to outgrow the shedding rate.
\end{itemize}

This discovery establishes an ecological rule of mucosal colonization: gut microbes resolve the colonization challenge through two divergent evolutionary strategies—either by investing in \textbf{mechanical attachment} or by investing in \textbf{metabolic specialization}.



\subsection{Latent metabolic directions and localized competitive pressure forecast host-specific responses to microbial interventions}
\label{subsec:german_intervention}

A pervasive challenge in clinical and agricultural microbiome interventions is the unpredictable, host-specific variability of treatment outcomes: an intervention that successfully establishes in one individual frequently fails entirely in another. 

To determine whether our low-dimensional CRM could explain and forecast such host-divergent outcomes, we investigated a complex livestock rumen microbiome dataset (Plata et al.~\cite{srinivasan2026low}, German dataset) comprising 81 prevalent bacterial and archaeal taxa cultured under baseline syngas ($5\,\text{bar}$) and two distinct chemical perturbations: Methanol ($\text{MeOH}$) and Trimethylamine ($\text{TMA}$). The experiments were performed using rumen fluid inocula derived from two genetically and phenotypically distinct dairy cattle hosts: Cow OSU and Cow BRRS.

Under the identical chemical intervention, the keystone methanogenic archaeon \textit{Methanomicrobium sp.} exhibited starkly divergent, host-specific behavior: in Cow BRRS, \textit{Methanomicrobium sp.} bloomed to dominate the community, whereas in Cow OSU, it was completely suppressed and failed to establish.

We applied our low-dimensional CRM ($K=3$) to this system to uncover the mechanistic basis of this divergence:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Unsupervised Discovery of Perturbation Coordinates:} Remarkably, without any biochemical labels or supervision, the learned initial resource capacities ($R_0$) in the latent space autonomously aligned with the experimental chemical amendments (Figure~\ref{fig:german_competition}A): Resource 0 remained constant across conditions, capturing the shared syngas base medium; Resource 1 expanded selectively under the Methanol perturbation ($\text{MeOH}$ axis); and Resource 2 expanded exclusively under the Trimethylamine perturbation ($\text{TMA}$ axis). The model independently discovered the chemical axes of the experiment.
    \item \textbf{Predicting Host Divergence from the Host-Specific (OSU-Trained) Model:} To test whether this divergence could be predicted from baseline community structure, we utilized the model trained strictly on the baseline host (Cow OSU). We computed the \textbf{localized competitive pressure} ($C_i$) exerted on \textit{Methanomicrobium sp.} by the resident community along its preferred latent resource axes:
\begin{equation}
C_i(t) = \sum_{j \ne i} \left( \sum_{k=1}^K \Theta_{ik} \Theta_{jk} R_k(t) \right) N_j(t).
\label{eq:competitive_pressure}
\end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Mechanistic Cause: Competitive Blockage versus Competitive Release:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item In Cow OSU, the resident community already harbored dense populations of methylotrophic competitors (\textit{Denitrobacterium detoxificans}, \textit{Bacteroides pyogenes}, and \textit{Thermoplasmata}) occupying the exact same metabolic niche coordinates ($\Theta_{\text{MeOH}}$, Figure~\ref{fig:german_competition}B--C). This generated an intense \textbf{competitive barrier} (competitive pressure = $5.80$ at $T_1$, increasing to $7.40$ at $T_6$; Figure~\ref{fig:german_competition}D), starving \textit{Methanomicrobium sp.} of resources and causing its competitive exclusion.
    \item In Cow BRRS, these specific competitors were depleted at baseline (Figure~\ref{fig:german_competition}C). The absence of niche competitors created a \textbf{niche vacuum} (competitive release), allowing \textit{Methanomicrobium sp.} to exploit the added methanol and expand rapidly.
\end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

By projecting complex microbiomes into low-dimensional latent resource coordinates, our model demonstrates that host-divergent intervention responses are not random stochastic anomalies; they are deterministic consequences of the geometric configuration of competitive pressure in the host's resident community.
